The capacitance of a cube

22 November 2026 Hrvoje Abraham, Fable 5.2 doi:10.5281/zenodo.24582301

A metal sphere of radius rrr has the capacitance 4πε0r4\pi\varepsilon_0 r4πε0​r. For a metal cube no formula is known, only numbers. This post has two series for that number, and two solvers for the second series.

The first series has positive terms and is made of charged spherical shells. Each term is an algebraic number from a closed formula, with no integral. The series is exact, but too slow to give many decimals.

The second series is fast. It has the same structure and is made of charged patches on the faces. The post explains why it is fast.

The first solver stores the matrix of the patch system and factors it, the Cholesky method. It gives 18 digits.

The second solver does not store the matrix, the conjugate gradient method. Each of its steps increases the lower bound. The post gives the two conditions for this, symmetry and positivity, and tells how the program keeps them in floating point. This solver gives two more digits:

The problem

Put the unit cube KKK at potential 1, in units where the potential of a point charge qqq at distance rrr is q/rq/rq/r. The charge moves to the surface and settles in an equilibrium distribution μ\muμ. Its total is the number κ\kappaκ. In these units a sphere of radius rrr holds the charge rrr, so κ\kappaκ is the radius of the sphere that is equivalent to the cube.

The density of μ\muμ is not known. It is infinite along the edges and still stronger at the corners, and this is the full difficulty of the problem.

One classical fact makes the problem approachable without the density. Take any charge distribution ν\nuν in the cube, not necessarily in equilibrium, with total charge q(ν)q(\nu)q(ν) and energy

E(ν)=∬dν(x) dν(y)∣x−y∣. E(\nu) = \iint \frac{d\nu(x)\, d\nu(y)}{|x-y|}. E(ν)=∬∣x−y∣dν(x)dν(y)​.

Then

2 q(ν)−E(ν)  =  κ−E(μ−ν)  ≤  κ. 2\,q(\nu) - E(\nu) \;=\; \kappa - E(\mu - \nu) \;\le\; \kappa . 2q(ν)−E(ν)=κ−E(μ−ν)≤κ.

The proof is one line. The potential of μ\muμ is 1 on the whole cube, thus the mutual energy of μ\muμ and ν\nuν is q(ν)q(\nu)q(ν), and E(μ−ν)=κ−2q(ν)+E(ν)E(\mu-\nu) = \kappa - 2q(\nu) + E(\nu)E(μ−ν)=κ−2q(ν)+E(ν) cannot be negative. Each guess ν\nuν gives a lower bound for κ\kappaκ, and the error of the bound is the energy of the error of the guess. A guess with an error of 10−810^{-8}10−8 gives κ\kappaκ to 10−1610^{-16}10−16.

The reason is the edges. Near an edge the density grows like d−1/3d^{-1/3}d−1/3, where ddd is the distance to the edge, and at a corner it grows faster, with an irrational power. To follow the density at distance ddd from an edge, the shells there must have a radius near ddd, and then 1/d1/d1/d of them are necessary along the edge. No arrangement of shells escapes this count.

Patches

The cure is to let the trial charges become thin. Put the charge on the faces, on rectangular patches that are a fixed fraction of the neighbour patch in the direction of each edge. Twenty-three layers reach from the centre of a face to 10−1310^{-13}10−13 from the edge. On each patch the density is a polynomial, of a higher degree on the large patches in the middle and of a low degree on the small ones near the edges.

The energies of two patches are fourfold integrals of 1/r1/r1/r, and they must be accurate to all digits. One identity makes them easy:

How the system is solved

Why HC=mHC = mHC=m? It is the same statement as Gq=1Gq = \mathbf 1Gq=1 for the shells. Add a small charge δ\deltaδ of the shape φaφb\varphi_a\varphi_bφa​φb​ to the faces. The bound 2q−E2q - E2q−E changes in proportion to δ (mab−(HC)ab)\delta\,(m_{ab} - (HC)_{ab})δ(mab​−(HC)ab​), and at the best CCC no such change can help, so all these differences are zero.

Physically, (HC)ab(HC)_{ab}(HC)ab​ is the potential of the full trial charge on a face, weighted with φaφb\varphi_a\varphi_bφa​φb​ and integrated, and mabm_{ab}mab​ is the same integral of the potential 1. So the system says: the surface is at potential 1, as far as the trial functions can test it. For the functions of degree 0 this means that the mean potential over each patch is exactly 1. The higher degrees also remove its mean slope and curvature over the patch. A conductor has the potential 1 at every point of its surface. The system asks for this in as many ways as there are unknowns. The matrix HHH is symmetric and positive definite, and it is full: each patch acts on each other patch. Why it is fast

The patch method has a name: it is the Galerkin boundary element method in its hp form. Each part of the name says one thing.

  • Boundary element. The unknown is the charge on the surface only, and the surface is divided into elements, here the patches. The equation is an integral equation: the potential of the surface charge is 1 on the surface. A finite element method has its unknowns in the full volume and solves a differential equation there. Its matrix is sparse, because only adjacent elements interact. Here the matrix is full, but the unknowns cover only a surface.
  • Galerkin. The equation is tested with the same functions that build the solution: one trial function, one equation. This is the system HC=mHC = mHC=m. It makes HHH symmetric, and it gives the lower bound.
  • h and p. The letter hhh is the size of the elements and ppp is the degree of the polynomials. The hhh version makes the elements smaller at a fixed degree. The ppp version increases the degree on a fixed mesh. The hphphp version does both: small elements of low degree where the solution is singular, large elements of high degree where it is smooth.

The Galerkin method with finite elements on a surface goes back to Nédélec and Planchard, Une méthode variationnelle d’éléments finis pour la résolution numérique d’un problème extérieur dans R3\mathbf R^3R3 (1973), and to Hsiao and Wendland, A finite element method for some integral equations of the first kind (1977). Uniform patches on the cube are older: Reitan and Higgins, Calculation of the electrical capacitance of a cube (1951), divided each face into 36 squares and found 0.65550.65550.6555. The hphphp idea is from Gui and Babuška, The h, p and h-p versions of the finite element method in 1 dimension (1986). Its exponential convergence for boundary elements was proved by Babuška, Guo and Stephan (1990) for polygons, and by Heuer, Maischak and Stephan, Exponential convergence of the hp-version for the boundary element method on open surfaces (1999), for surfaces in space. A modern textbook is Sauter and Schwab, Boundary Element Methods (2011).

Matrix-free conjugate gradient

The Cholesky method stores the matrix HHH. For MMM unknowns it has M2M^2M2 entries: 28 GB for the largest run above, and a computer with 64 GB stops near 85 000 unknowns. The work of the factorization grows like M3M^3M3. But the matrix is not necessary.

The two conditions. The method converges strictly on two conditions. Strictly means that the bound of each step is above the bound of the step before it, and that no bound is above κM\kappa_MκM​, the value of the full system. The conditions are:

  1. Symmetry. DTHC=CTHDD^{\mathsf T} H C = C^{\mathsf T} H DDTHC=CTHD for each two trial charges CCC and DDD.

A matrix with the two properties is named symmetric positive definite. Here the two are statements about energy. The number 6 DTHC6\,D^{\mathsf T} H C6DTHC is the mutual energy of the charges CCC and DDD, and a mutual energy does not depend on the order of the two charges. The number 6 CTHC6\,C^{\mathsf T} H C6CTHC is the energy of the charge CCC, and the energy of a charge is positive. But the system of the recipe does not have the two properties by itself. The programs

The three programs are plain Julia with threads, without packages:

  • shells.jl makes the two shell tables. The matrix is assembled over the orbits of the 48 symmetries. The values QnQ_nQn​ are refined to 28 digits with double-double residuals, so that the extrapolation does not amplify rounding errors.
  • patches.jl makes the patch table. The integrals in one variable cancel strongly and use 1024-bit numbers. The matrix HHH and its Cholesky factor are in double precision, and the solution is refined with double-double residuals.
  • cg.jl solves the same system with the conjugate gradient method. It includes patches.jl for the mesh and the tables, and it adds the products without the matrix, the steps in double precision and the residual in double-double.

Notes

Is this new? The idea is not. It is the Rayleigh–Ritz method: restrict a variational problem to a finite family and solve a linear system. Each part has a name and a history.

  • The energy principle is due to Gauss and Thomson. For the capacity it is the base of Pólya and Szegő, Isoperimetric Inequalities in Mathematical Physics (1951).
  • A finite family with the matrix of energies is Maxwell’s method of subareas, in electrical engineering the method of moments (Harrington, 1968), and with the energy as the scalar product the Galerkin boundary element method. The formula 1TG−11\mathbf 1^{\mathsf T} G^{-1} \mathbf 11TG−11 with its error identity is in Betcke, Haberl and Praetorius, Adaptive boundary element methods for the computation of the electrostatic capacity on complex polyhedra (2019).
  • The energy of two overlapping shells is, up to a factor, the trace of the Rotne–Prager–Yamakawa tensor of hydrodynamics (Zuk, Wajnryb, Mizerski and Szymczak, 2014). There the same sum over the inverse matrix gives the hydrodynamic radius of a chain of beads.
  • G=LLTG = LL^{\mathsf T}G=LLT and ∑zj2\sum z_j^2∑zj2​ are Cholesky, Gram–Schmidt and Parseval.
  • Patches graded in a fixed ratio toward the edges, with polynomials on them, are the hp boundary element method, which is known to converge exponentially for such densities. The references are in the section Why it is fast.
  • The conjugate gradient method is from Hestenes and Stiefel (1952). A solver that uses only products with the matrix is the usual way to solve a large boundary element system. That its steps give lower bounds of mTH−1mm^{\mathsf T}H^{-1}mmTH−1m, and how to get upper bounds, is in Golub and Meurant (1997, 2010).
  • The sum of Gaussians for 1/r1/r1/r is a standard tool as well: sinc quadrature (Stenger), and Beylkin and Monzón, Approximation by exponential sums revisited (2010).

What can be particular to this post is the combination: the nested shell family in this order as an explicit series, and the patch system with all integrals reduced to erf⁡\operatorname{erf}erf. I did not find the shell series in the literature, and a short search showed no published value of κ\kappaκ with more than 14 digits. Neither search was complete, and I do not claim that anything here is new.

The lower bounds are exact statements about exact arithmetic. That the bounds converge to κ\kappaκ, and how many digits are final, rests here on the numbers: on the agreement of runs with different meshes, degrees and parameters, on the agreement of the two solvers, and on the agreement with the 13 digits of Helsing and Perfekt, On the polarizability and capacitance of the cube (2013), 0.660678154099570.660678154099570.66067815409957.
